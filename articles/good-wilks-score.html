What Is a Good Wilks Score?

Published 25 October 2026 · 7 min read

The Wilks score exists to answer a fairness question: how strong is this lifter, once bodyweight is taken out of the equation? A 60 kg lifter totalling 400 kg and a 100 kg lifter totalling 500 kg are both strong, but in relative terms they are not equally strong. Wilks converts each total into a single comparable number. This guide explains what the score measures, the ranges lifters commonly cite from beginner to elite, and — more usefully — what actually moves your number.

What the Wilks score actually measures

Wilks takes your total — squat plus bench plus deadlift, or bench-only in single-lift variants — multiplies it by a bodyweight coefficient, and produces a score. The coefficient is largest for lighter lifters and decreases as bodyweight rises, which compensates for the fact that strength grows with bodyweight, but not in a perfectly straight line. Two lifters with identical Wilks scores have, in a statistical sense, equally impressive totals for their size.

The formula was created by Robert Wilks of Powerlifting Australia and became the standard coefficient system across many federations from the mid-1990s. A revised version, usually called Wilks 2020, updated the underlying coefficients after researchers flagged distortions at the extremes of bodyweight. If you are comparing scores across years or federations, check which version produced them — the revision shifted numbers by a few percent, more for some bodyweights than others.

A worked example

Using Wilks 2020: a male lifter at 100 kg bodyweight with a 700 kg total scores about 510 Wilks. The same total at the same bodyweight under the original Wilks would land somewhat differently — which is why the version matters. A female lifter at 60 kg totalling 400 kg scores about 528 Wilks on the updated coefficients.

Scores near 500 represent genuinely competitive regional-level lifting. The scale does not have a ceiling or floor — it simply rescales totals so they can be compared across bodyweights.

So what is "good"? Commonly cited ranges

Wilks has no official ladder of ranks — "good" depends on federation, population, and purpose. That said, the lifting community has converged on rough bands that are useful for goal-setting. Treat these as orientation, not standards:

Wilks (approx.)Common descriptionWhat it usually reflects
Under 250BeginnerEarly training years; technique still consolidating
250–300NoviceConsistent training; first real strength base
300–350IntermediateMultiple serious training blocks behind you
350–400AdvancedCompetitive at local and many regional meets
400–450High-level amateurStrong at regional level; national meet territory
450+ElitePodium contention at national and international meets

Two caveats belong next to any table like this. First, these bands are community convention, not federation standards — check your own federation's qualifying totals for anything official. Second, scoring systems differ: DOTS and IPF GL points produce different numbers for the same lifter, and comparing a Wilks score to a DOTS score directly is a category error.

Wilks vs DOTS vs IPF GL points

Wilks is no longer the only — or even the most common — coefficient system. The IPF replaced Wilks with its own Gates-level (GL) points in 2020, and many raw meets now use DOTS, which is simpler and statistically better behaved at bodyweight extremes. All three systems do the same job: adjust a total for bodyweight. If your gym debates scores, first agree which system produced them.

How to actually raise your Wilks

Raise the total — that is the lever

Wilks improves in exactly one way: your total goes up faster than your bodyweight. That means the same fundamentals that build any lifter — progressive overload across squat, bench, and deadlift, sensible volume, and patient technique work. There is no Wilks-specific training trick.

Do not chase the score through bodyweight alone

Cutting weight purely to shrink the denominator is a poor trade outside a genuine competition context. You perform and recover worse, the total usually drops with you, and the math rarely pays. Let bodyweight follow your training, not the reverse.

Attack the weak lift

Your total rises fastest when your weakest of the three lifts gets attention. Our Weak-Point Finder and Program Builder are built for exactly that: identify the lagging lift, give it a block of focused volume, and watch the coefficient arithmetic do the flattering.

Keep the scoring version straight

When you log scores over months, note which formula and version produced each number. Tracking Wilks 2020 against an old Wilks chart will make progress look different than it is — consistency in scoring is as important as consistency in training.

Common questions

Is 300 Wilks good?

By the common bands above, 300 sits at the entry to intermediate: a real training base and a total that holds up at your first meets. It is a normal two-to-four-year milestone for consistent lifters, and a fine first big goal.

What Wilks do elite lifters hit?

Podium-level national lifters commonly land in the 450+ range, and world-class performances push past 500. Rather than anchoring on the elite band, anchor on your own trend — the score only moves when the total moves.

Why did my Wilks change when the formula did not?

Because bodyweight did. Wilks is sensitive to both sides of the ratio: the same total at a lower bodyweight scores higher, and the coefficient curve is not linear, so small weight changes can nudge the score more than you expect.

Conclusion: a compass, not a scoreboard

A "good" Wilks score is the one above where you were last quarter. The bands from beginner to elite give you a map of the territory, and the 250 / 300 / 350 / 400 milestones give you something to aim at. Pick your scoring system, keep it consistent, train the weak lift, and let the coefficient arithmetic translate your total into a number that means the same thing at any bodyweight.
