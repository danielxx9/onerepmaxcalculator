Epley vs. Brzycki: Which 1RM Formula Is Most Accurate?

Published 30 October 2026 · 11 min read

Type any weight and rep count into a one rep max calculator and you will notice the answers disagree. That is because every calculator runs prediction formulas, and the two most famous one-rep max formulas, the Epley formula and the Brzycki formula, were built by different coaches 30 years apart. This guide shows you the math behind both, where they agree, where they fight, and which one to trust for your training weights.

As the person who coded the math inside this site's calculators, I have a soft spot for this argument: the first time I plotted Epley against Brzycki for my own lifts, the gap at three reps was barely worth a sentence, and at twelve reps it was a real disagreement about fatigue. Once I understood that both formulas are curve fits with different opinions about rep quality, I stopped treating the spread as a bug and started programming from the consensus. This guide is the explanation I wish someone had handed me before I wrote version one of the calculator.

What Is a 1RM Prediction Formula?

A 1RM formula estimates your one rep max from a submaximal set. You give it two inputs, the load and the rep count, and it returns the weight you could theoretically lift once. The assumption underneath is simple: as reps go up, the weight you can handle goes down along a predictable curve.

Coach Boyd Epley published his version at the University of Nebraska in 1985 as a poundage chart for his football players. The Epley formula reads: 1RM = weight Ãƒ— (1 + reps/30). Every 30 reps worth of fatigue is assumed to cost you one full bar's worth of load, which makes the relationship linear and easy to run in your head.

Instead of a straight line, Brzycki used a curve that bends upward as reps climb, reflecting what he saw with college lifters on the bench press and other lifts.

Strength coaches in powerlifting and Olympic weightlifting had used load-rep charts for years; these two stuck because the math was simple enough to run at the rack. Neither formula came from a lab — both came from practical coaching, and both have held up for decades because they work reasonably well at the rep counts coaches actually use. The fight is not about whether they work; it is about when each one is most accurate.

Where They Agree and Where They Fight

Here is the part most articles miss: Epley and Brzycki are not rivals across the board. They agree exactly at 10 reps, where both return a multiplier of 1.333, meaning your estimated max is a third higher than the 10-rep weight. Below 10 reps Brzycki reads lower and plays it conservative, while above 10 it reads higher than Epley and keeps climbing faster.

At 3 reps the seven formulas disagree by about 8%, which is noise you can program around; at 15 reps they disagree by nearly 25%, which is the difference between a training max and a fantasy. The 1RM calculation is only as honest as the rep count you feed it.

Run a concrete case and the divergence stops being abstract. Lift 100 kg for 15 reps and Epley returns 150.00 kg while Brzycki returns 163.64 kg, a 13.6 kg gap from identical data. Research adds that the classic percentage tables behind both curves are approximations rather than laws (Schoenfeld et al., 2021) — the formulas disagree most exactly where the evidence says to trust them least.

What the Research Says About Accuracy

Laboratory studies have tested these equations against real max attempts, and the pattern is consistent. Reynolds and colleagues measured lifters across multiple rep ranges and found that predictions from low-rep sets matched measured maxes far better than predictions from high-rep sets, with errors growing as the rep count climbed (Reynolds et al., 2006). A set of 3 to 5 reps is where the formulas live comfortably.

LeSuer and colleagues tested seven prediction equations against actual 1RM performances on the bench press, back squat, and deadlift in 1997 (LeSuer et al., 1997), and the Epley formula ranked among the most accurate across all three lifts at low rep counts. Their work is a big reason Epley became the default in strength training software and textbooks. Brzycki did not lose; it simply behaves differently by design, sitting more conservative at exactly the rep ranges where Epley leans optimistic.

Formulas also assume crisp reps, and effort changes everything. Research on the RPE scale shows that lifters can rate how many reps they had left with useful accuracy, which lets you adjust estimates for grinders and easy sets alike (Zourdos et al., 2016). A 5-rep set at RPE 8 is calculator food; a 5-rep set at RPE 10 is a different animal, and the formula will flatter you.

Which Formula Should You Use?

For most lifters, most of the time, the honest answer is both: the Epley formula is the better default at very low rep counts, because research has repeatedly validated it there. The Brzycki formula is the better guardrail when you are using a heavier rep count like 6-8 and want a conservative number for programming. When the two disagree by a kilogram or two, split the difference.

That is exactly why our 1RM calculator runs seven formulas at once and shows a consensus estimate next to each individual result. Consensus beats any single equation for the same reason a panel of judges beats a single judge. If your Epley and Brzycki numbers sit within a couple percent, trust the range; if they are far apart, your rep count was too high or the set was not honest.

Match the formula to the goal as well. A powerlifter peaking for a max lifts day wants the aggressive end of the range because their max matters, while a lifter chasing muscle hypertrophy only needs a rough number to set their 70-75% work, so the conservative end does the job. Muscular endurance blocks care even less, since 60% work stays safe under any reasonable estimate.

Meet the Rest of the Formula Family

Epley and Brzycki get the headlines, but the other equations in your one rep max calculator earn their keep. Wathan and Lander came from laboratory curve-fitting, and their exponential models track measured strength drop-offs closely across a wide rep range. O'Conner's formula, weight times one plus 2.5% per rep, is the simplest of the set and reads conservative by design.

The Lombardi formula takes a different route entirely, raising the rep count to the power of 0.10. That power curve stays flatter than Brzycki's at high reps, which is why it often lands closest to reality on longer sets. None of these is a magic answer, and any estimated 1RM should be read next to the others rather than alone.

Practical use is simple. In powerlifting and weightlifting circles, the low-rep classics rule. In general strength training, the consensus across all seven 1RM formulas beats any single one, because each carries a small bias in a different direction.

Step-by-Step: Testing a Rep Max for a Clean Estimate

  1. Pick the lift and the target. Bench press 1RM and squat 1RM estimates behave best; the deadlift 1RM responds well too as long as your setup is consistent.
  2. Choose a weight for 3-5 reps. Around 80-87% of your current estimated max should land you there. A spotter or rack safeties still apply, because rep maxes get heavy.
  3. Perform the set with crisp reps. Same tempo, full range of motion, no grinders. Stop at RPE 8 with one or two reps left.
  4. Rest, then run the numbers. Enter load and rep count into the calculator and read the full spread. Note the consensus, not just one formula.
  5. Sanity-check against history. Your new estimated 1RM should sit within a few percent of your last one unless you just ran a training block. Wild swings mean bad data, not sudden strength gains.
  6. Write it down and program from it. Set your training max, load your percentages, and re-estimate in four to six weeks.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Feeding the formula 12-15 rep sets. Past 10 reps, formula spread explodes toward 25%. High-rep sets measure muscular endurance, not maximal strength.
  • Grinding to failure and counting it. Formulas assume honest reps. A grinder at RPE 10 inflates your estimate by kilos you cannot actually lift.
  • Switching formulas every month. Pick the consensus approach and stay with it, or your trend line becomes fiction.
  • Mixing up kilograms and pounds. Formulas are unit-agnostic but you are not. Enter the units you actually loaded.
  • Comparing estimates across lifts without context. A bench press max and a deadlift max respond differently to the same equation. Judge each lift against its own history.
  • Treating the estimate as a promise. It is a planning number with a few percent of error. Program with a margin, not a wish.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which is more accurate, Epley or Brzycki?

At 3-5 reps they land within 2-4% of each other, so both are accurate enough, and the seven-formula consensus beats either alone. Epley has slightly better validation at very low rep counts, while Brzycki runs more conservative below 10 reps. Use the consensus instead of betting on one.

Why do different one rep max calculators give different answers?

Calculators run different equations under the hood, and each equation models the load-rep curve differently. Differences of 2-5% at low rep counts are normal. If two calculators disagree by more than 10%, you entered a high rep count, and the math itself is warning you.

Do the formulas work for exercises other than the big lifts?

They were built on barbell lifts and are most trustworthy there: bench press, back squat, deadlift, front squat, and overhead press. Machine and dumbbell estimates run less true because of mechanical advantage and stabilization differences. The further from a barbell, the more the estimate is a rough guide.

Conclusion and Next Steps

Epley is the optimistic veteran, Brzycki is the cautious accountant, and they agree almost perfectly at 10 reps. Feed either one a 3-5 rep set with clean technique and you get a dependable training number; feed them a 15-rep grinder and you get noise dressed up as data. Run the consensus, set your training max a touch conservative, and let the next training block, not the calculator, decide how strong you are.
