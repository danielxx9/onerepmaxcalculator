How to Calculate and Increase Your Bench Press 1RM

Published 30 October 2026 · 11 min read

The bench press is the lift everyone asks about, and your bench press 1RM is the number that answers it. This guide shows you two honest ways to calculate that max, the form checks that make the number real, and a percentage-based plan that turns a decent bench into a big one.

My bench sat at 115 kg for the better part of a year while I repped to failure every Friday and called it hard work. Two fixes moved it: running pressing days off calculated percentages like the plan below, and treating the paused touch as a form standard instead of an option — the bar climbed to my current 130 kg within two training blocks. Everything in this guide comes from that rebuild.

Why the Bench Press 1RM Is the Number Everyone Cares About

"How much do you bench?" is the most famous question in the gym, and the bench press 1RM is the only honest answer. It is the heaviest barbell you can press from your chest to full lockout for one clean repetition, and it is the standard measure of chest strength and upper body pushing power.

The lift earns that reputation because it is measurable. Same bar height, same plate arithmetic, same rules from one session to the next — that consistency is exactly why barbell training works. The bench press is also the second event in powerlifting competition, and OpenPowerlifting hosts millions of real competition results you can browse to see how bench numbers scale with bodyweight and training age.

Your max matters beyond bragging rights, too. Every serious pressing program is written in percentages, and What Is a One Rep Max? explains how those prescriptions turn a single number into every working set you do — know the max, and the whole program snaps into focus.

How to Calculate Your Bench Press 1RM: True Test or Estimate

You have two options. Option one is a true max test: ramp up over five to seven warm-up sets, set your feet, and attempt a single heavy repetition with a spotter or safety pins in place. The full ramp-up percentages, rest windows, and spotting plan are covered in How to Test Your 1RM Safely.

Option two is the estimated 1RM, and it is what most lifters should use most weeks. You press a submaximal set of 3-5 clean reps, note the load and rep count, and let a prediction formula do the rest — that is exactly what a one rep max calculator bench press estimate gives you.

Here is the math in action. Suppose you bench 100 kg for 5 solid reps: the Epley formula returns 116.67 kg, the Brzycki formula returns 112.50 kg, and the Lombardi formula returns 117.46 kg. Those three estimates sit within about 5 kg of each other, and the consensus of roughly 115.5 kg is plenty accurate for programming.

The 1RM calculator runs all seven classic equations at once and shows the consensus automatically. If you want to see how the formulas differ and why they drift apart at higher rep counts, Epley vs. Brzycki and How to Calculate Your 1RM cover the full derivation.

Bench Press Form Checks Before You Load the Bar

A max is only as good as the reps it is built on, so run these form checks every set, from warm-ups to the big single. NASM, the National Academy of Sports Medicine, teaches the same sequence in its exercise library: set your feet flat, pinch your shoulder blades down and back, brace, and press the bar to lockout over your shoulders.

The five checks that matter most: grip the bar so your forearms stay vertical at the bottom, lower it to your lower chest under control, keep your hips on the bench, drive your feet into the floor, and finish each rep at full lockout. Skip any one of them and the number stops counting as a bench press 1RM.

Grip width deserves its own check. Research on grip width found that changing hand spacing shifts muscle activation between the chest, shoulders, and triceps and changes how much you can lift, with a medium grip measuring strongest for most trained lifters (Saeterbakken et al., 2021). Pick the width that lets the bar touch your chest with forearms vertical, then keep it consistent.

One more standard makes every number cleaner: touch the chest and pause for a beat before pressing. Imposing a pause between the lowering and pressing phases has been shown to make bench press strength testing more reliable, because it removes bounce and momentum from the measurement (Pallarés et al., 2014). The Sticking Point: Where Bench Press Maxes Go to Die

A few centimeters above your chest, the bar suddenly stops moving. That spot is the sticking point, and nearly every failed max attempt dies right there, in the first third of the press.

Researchers studying the bench press load-velocity relationship found that the sticking region sits in the early ascent, and that the range of motion you use changes the whole relationship: partial reps let the bar move faster at the same load, which makes the lift easier and inflates your estimated numbers (Martínez-Cava et al., 2019). Half-repping a max is not a max.

The fix is boring and effective: press through the sticking point on purpose. Pause bench presses kill the bounce and build strength exactly where the bar stalls, close-grip work shifts load onto the triceps that finish the lockout, and a stronger back keeps the bar path tight. Train the weak slice of the lift and the whole max climbs.

How to Increase Your Bench Press 1RM: The Percentages

Strength grows through progressive overload, the principle the American College of Sports Medicine has anchored its resistance training guidance on for decades (ACSM, 2009). In practice, that means pressing against percentages of your 1RM that change across a training block. Here is the loading table the bench responds to:

% of bench 1RM Typical reps What it builds
95-100%1-2Competition skill, peaking, true max attempts
90%2-4Maximal strength
80-85%4-6Strength and power
70-75%8-12Muscle hypertrophy, chest and triceps size
60-65%12-20Muscular endurance, pressing work capacity

The heavy end of that table is non-negotiable: a meta-analysis found that training above 60% of your max produces significantly greater 1RM gains than lighter loads, even when muscle growth is similar (Schoenfeld et al., 2017). To press more, you have to practice pressing heavy.

Frequency is the second lever. A meta-analysis of training frequency research found that training a lift more than once per week produced greater strength gains than a single weekly session (Grgic et al., 2018). Two bench days — one heavy, one moderate — beat one max-out Friday almost every time.

Program from a training max rather than your raw max: take 85-90% of your true or estimated 1RM, and calculate every percentage from that. If your consensus estimate is 115.5 kg, your training max is about 104 kg, so an 80% set of 4-6 is a 92.5 kg working weight — the plate calculator tells you which plates make that number. The cushion keeps hard weeks honest and bar speed sharp.

Autoregulation keeps the percentages useful on bad days. Rating your reps in reserve and adjusting the load accordingly matches what lifters actually have on a given day, and the RIR-based RPE system was validated against bar velocity in the bench press and squat (Zourdos et al., 2016).

Finally, train the speed side of the lift. Explosive work such as bench press throws develops upper body power, and studies show that priming the press with a heavy isometric hold before throwing can further boost power output (Koźlenia et al., 2025). Accessory Work That Adds Kilos to the Bar

Three muscles press the bar: the pectorals, the front delts, and the triceps, and building them is how you raise the ceiling. Muscle hypertrophy at 70-75% of your max for sets of 8-12 grows the engine that heavy singles express, which is why size and strength cycles alternate rather than compete.

Pick accessories that map onto the press itself. Close-grip bench presses and dips load the triceps that finish your lockout, overhead presses build the front delts that stabilize the bar at the bottom, and heavy rows protect the shoulder girdle while giving your arch something solid to press off. Two or three accessory lifts per bench day is enough.

Keep the accessories submaximal and stop each set with one or two reps left in the tank. Chasing failure on every accessory set piles fatigue onto the exact joints your max depends on, and tired shoulders press small numbers.

Common Bench Press Max Mistakes

  • Bouncing the bar off your chest. The bounce does the pressing for you, so the result is not your bench. Touch, pause, then press.
  • Cutting the range of motion. A max that never touches the chest is a board press. It inflates your ego and your calculator input, then breaks on test day.
  • Maxing out every week. True singles at 95-100% tax the elbows, shoulders, and nervous system. Test every 8-12 weeks and estimate in between.
  • Benching without a spotter or safety pins. A pinned bar on your chest is an emergency with no plan. Set the rack before the attempt, never after.
  • Flaring the elbows straight out. Elbows at a 90-degree angle to the torso concentrate stress on the shoulder joint. Tuck them to roughly 45-70 degrees and the press gets stronger and safer at once.
  • Judging your max against the wrong bodyweight. A 100 kg bench means something different at 70 kg than at 110 kg. Compare fairly with a Wilks and DOTS calculator before you rank yourself against anyone.

Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate is a one rep max calculator bench press estimate?

Bench press estimates usually land within 5% of your true max when you feed the calculator a crisp 3-5 rep set. The lift's stable range of motion makes it one of the best movements for prediction. Accuracy fades past 10 reps, where grinding and slowing bar speed distort the math.

How long does it take to increase my bench press 1RM?

Beginners often add several kilograms per month, while intermediate lifters gain 2-5% over a full training block. Advanced lifters may fight for a single kilogram across a whole season. Program from an estimated max, train the percentages, and retest every 8-12 weeks to keep the numbers honest.

Can you test a bench press 1RM without a spotter?

Only with safety pins set just below chest height inside a power rack. A failed bench pins the bar on the chest or throat, and injury surveys in strength sports show the pectorals and shoulders take most of the bench-related damage. A rack plus a spotter is the professional standard.

Conclusion and Next Steps

Your bench press 1RM is two things at once: a scoreboard and a set of dials. Calculate it honestly with a strict 3-5 rep estimate, retest it a few times a year with a spotter, and let the percentages run every set in between. Add a second bench day, train the sticking point on purpose, and feed the accessories.
